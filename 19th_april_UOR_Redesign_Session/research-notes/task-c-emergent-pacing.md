# Task C — Emergent Narrative Techniques & Pacing Ongoing Stories

Research notes for the UOR redesign. Two interlocking angles:
- **A.** What authored content can steal from emergent games to make scripted story *feel* emergent.
- **B.** How ongoing stories sustain tension without a fixed climax — horizon management, nested arcs, renewing dramatic questions.

## Findings — Angle A: Making Authored Feel Emergent

### A1. The "dramatic curve" engine — wave scheduling, not monotonic escalation [1][27]
RimWorld's AI storytellers do not ramp tension upward forever. Cassandra Classic maps events to an Aristotelian curve: exposition → rising action → climax → **catastrophe + recovery**, then restart. Phoebe Chillax lengthens the calm lanes. The crucial mechanic: **after any peak event, future threat rolls are damped for a fixed window** so the player perceives breathing room before the next push [27]. Phoebe is literally "increased downtime between events." [27]

> "She will throw challenges of increasing complexity at the player but in a measured manner, allowing time in between events for recovery." [27]

**Pattern for authored content:** tag every scripted beat with a `tension_weight` and a `recovery_cost`. After a high-weight beat fires, no other high-weight beat is eligible until the player has consumed N low-weight ambient beats. This is the inverse of the Freytag pyramid — it's a **sine wave that loops**, designed to never exhaust.

### A2. Wealth-linked difficulty — the world pushes back on success, not the clock [27]
RimWorld's "Storyteller Wealth" ties incoming threat to the colony's accumulated wealth rather than elapsed time. Prosperity is the punishment-multiplier. **The game feels emergent because consequences look causal** — you got rich, therefore raiders. The curve only looks dramatic because the player's own choices drive the slope.

**Pattern for authored:** gate escalation on *player-earned state* (reputation, hoard, territory, bonds), not on time or quest count. The scripted Mob Boss scene feels earned because the player's conspicuous wealth summoned it.

### A3. AI Director ebb-and-flow with explicit phase labels [8]
Valve's Left 4 Dead director, which Sylvester credits as RimWorld's ancestor [27], runs in **three named phases — Build Up → Peak → Relax — with stop conditions** [8]. During Relax, zombies literally stop spawning so the player's nervous system can down-regulate. Peak and Relax are not emergent accidents; they're server-enforced quiet windows where the author ensures relief.

**Pattern:** every authored scene sequence should have a **relax token** — a passage of guaranteed mechanical quiet where the player can read flavor, tidy inventory, and "come down" before the next push begins. Skipping the relax token is how VN pacing feels exhausting even when individual scenes are good.

### A4. Emergence detection — the game labels accidents as story [6][7]
Fåhraeus's GDC 2014 talk calls out a design principle: when the simulation throws up a lucky collision (two scripted events gluing together accidentally), the engine **scans for that pattern, re-weights similar coincidences, and uses music/text to foreground it** [6][7]. Players perceive the whole thing as authored fate.

> "Humans are good at seeing patterns in time [and] have a tendency to put meaning where there is none." [7]

**Pattern for authored:** add a pass after random event selection that **looks for thematic resonance with recent events** (same NPC, same location, same flag) and rewrites the surface text slightly to echo what just happened. Cheap at runtime, enormous perceived coherence.

### A5. Generate history before the player arrives, then let discovery be the narrative [9][10]
Caves of Qud builds five Sultans' biographies in full before the game starts, then seeds the world with "legends, forgotten tapestry, and ruined statues" as apophenic fuel [10]. Grinblat's stated design: "we wanted the setting's present to have richness, to be more than a collection of arrow-hints pointing toward the conditions that produced it." [10]

**Pattern:** for an ongoing authored game, **pre-generate a hidden backstory layer** (NPC secrets, past relationships, buried grudges) and let encounters *reveal* rather than *introduce*. The same trick makes a 200-choice story feel like a 2000-choice world because discovery ≠ content.

### A6. Adams's "story engine" is actually a fact generator with player-side narration [3][4][5]
Tarn Adams explicitly frames Dwarf Fortress as a **writing prompt**, not a story:

> "Your own mind is a story-building engine, and we can help that." [5]

The game emits facts (a dwarf drowned; a vampire fed; a goblin became a legendary lyre-player in 847). **Narrative is the player's re-stitching of those facts** in the aftermath. This is why emergent play feels authored — the player is the author [3][4].

**Pattern for authored:** surface *fact logs* to the player as first-class UI — birthdays, relationship deltas, trait changes, overheard gossip — even when those facts have no immediate mechanical effect. The logs are narrative scaffolding; the player's brain does the rest.

### A7. The Sims's "wishes + autonomy + collision" recipe [11][12]
Jenkins calls The Sims the canonical emergent narrative case: conflicting desires, legible spatial affordances, consequential systems. Sims decide autonomously what they want to do; the player reacts [11]. The drama comes from **collisions between autonomy and player intent** — the sim who keeps flirting with the gardener while you're trying to run a wedding.

**Pattern for authored:** give NPCs autonomous micro-desires that fire on a schedule independent of the plot. Most of the time nothing interesting happens. Occasionally their desire collides with the player's active goal and produces the feeling "my NPCs have minds of their own."

### A8. Jenkins's four architectures — a menu, not a ladder [12]
Jenkins labels four narrative architectures: **evocative spaces, enacting stories, embedded narratives, emergent narratives** [12]. Most ongoing authored games only use enacting. Adding embedded (backstory in environment) and evocative (genre priors you don't have to explain) is the cheapest way to look bigger than you are. Emergent is expensive to build but compatible with the other three — they stack.

## Findings — Angle B: Pacing Ongoing Stories Without a Climax

### B1. The Schell interest curve is *fractal*, not a single rise [16]
Schell's "interest curve" is not one rise-and-climax. It is **nested curves at every scale** — moment, scene, level, act, whole game — each with its own hook, dips, and payoff [16]. A never-ending game is just the outermost curve being open-ended while the inner curves continue to close.

**Nested-arc stack for ongoing authored stories:**
| Horizon | Loop duration | What closes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beat | 30s–3min | A single exchange or decision | Scene choice resolves |
| Scene | 5–15min | One micronarrative | "Tonight I paid rent" |
| Session | 30–90min | A day/week of in-game time | "This week I made $500 and the landlord left me alone" |
| Chapter | 3–10 sessions | A mid-term question | "Will the heat bar ever cool?" |
| Act | 10–30 sessions | A lifestyle shift | "I moved out of the motel" |
| Lifetime | Open | Identity transformation | Player's character-sheet drift |

The key: each horizon must have its own **opening → escalation → closure**, and they must be offset so that at any given moment at least one horizon is mid-rise and at least one is at closure.

### B2. The "renewing question" — lap-over arcs instead of climaxes [25][26]
Soap operas keep 50-year runs breathing by introducing a **new central question before the old one closes**. Nothing resolves cleanly — today's vindication plants tomorrow's jealous-employee setup [25]:

> "What separates the soap opera is that nothing ever is fully solved — he may be professing his love to her today, but his eye will wander to another stiletto walker next month, and the company may have cleared its name today but there's another jealous employee plotting revenge." [25]

Head writers plan months ahead, outlining **major beats and turning points** in advance, and deliberately interlock arcs so one arc's climax *triggers* the next arc's inciting incident [25]. Agnes Nixon's All My Children was designed with the story bible first, then written forward with deliberate social-issue substitution (Vietnam, AIDS) as the renewable dramatic fuel [26].

**Pattern:** never let the player reach a state where all active dramatic questions are resolved simultaneously. As Question A approaches resolution, spawn Question B at lower intensity. The overlap produces the soap opera's distinctive *always-mid-something* feel.

### B3. Quality-based narrative kills the "ran out of content" wall [17][18]
Short's storylet definition: content + prerequisites + effects [18]. The content pool doesn't need to be consumed linearly — storylets unlock and re-become eligible based on *qualities* (state variables). The same storylet pool can serve 5 hours or 500 hours because the **selection surface changes faster than the catalog does** [17][18].

> "Storylets are simple, atomic, robust, and recombinable... they support games of any size, meant to last for years, to include DLC." [18]

Three selection models:
- **Quality-based (QBN, Fallen London)** — player sees all eligible storylets, picks
- **Salience-based (Short 2016)** — drama manager picks the *most relevant* based on current world state
- **Waypoint** — NPC pathfinds across dialogue topics toward an authored beat; "the story engine is constantly trying to heal the story, to move from the player's chaotic input back towards the next authored beat." [17]

**Pattern:** for ongoing authored content, drop the scene graph and convert scenes into storylets with prerequisite expressions. Let selection be a *query* against current state. Adding a single new quality (e.g. `bar_reputation > 3`) reshuffles the entire narrative surface without rewriting any scenes.

### B4. Short's pacing grammar — alternate linear, freeform, and cost [19]
Short diagrams storylet structures as three shapes [19]:
- **Linear sections** (squares): tightly controlled, reserved for climactic moments
- **Freeform wheels**: player has high freedom but low intensity, used for exploration / grinding / relationship accrual
- **Cost elements**: gate passage by consuming resources gathered elsewhere — explicitly a "slowing feature"

> "Areas where the player has a lot of freedom tend to feel less intense; more linear, constrained sections are good for climactic moments." [19]

She warns against wheels-leading-to-wheels ("slow and sloggy") and recommends **interrupting freeform runs with a mandatory story-specific storylet** every so often — a forced beat that breaks the grind and reminds the player the story is moving [19].

**Pattern:** tag each storylet as linear / freeform / cost. Enforce ratios at runtime. After X freeform triggers in a row, force a linear one — regardless of what the player clicked on.

### B5. Loops carry long-running engagement; arcs get consumed [20]
Cook: an arc is "a broken loop you exit immediately." Loops sustain through mastery development. Evergreen games run on loops wrapped in disposable arcs [20]. **The ongoing game problem is identical to the MMO endgame problem** — once the arcs are spent, what remains?

**Pattern:** every ongoing authored story needs at least one deep loop underneath (skill mastery, relationship cultivation, economic optimization, collection) that continues to produce novelty after all scripted arcs are exhausted. The arcs are the hook; the loop is the retention.

### B6. Fallen London's action economy — pacing baked into the clock [28]
Fallen London paces a years-long narrative by **rationing actions** — 20 at a time, refilling one per 10 minutes [28]. This is not monetization theatre; it's the pacing mechanism. The drip-feed *enforces* breathing room, forces the player to savor each storylet, and guarantees no player can burn through the content in a weekend [28].

Menaces (Nightmares, Suspicion, Wounds, Scandal) add a second rhythm: any high-intensity storylet raises a menace, and when a menace hits 8 the player is **forcibly relocated to a penalty location** where they must spend actions reducing it [28]. The system auto-generates pace by converting narrative intensity into a queue of recovery storylets.

**Pattern:** impose either a time-gated action budget *or* an intensity-debt system where high-drama scenes automatically schedule mandatory cool-down scenes. The game paces itself; the player can't burn out.

### B7. Destiny 2's weekly drip — and why it broke [21][22][23]
Destiny 2's best-received seasons (Chosen, Splicer) ran a **three-act structure stretched across ~13 weeks**: setup weeks → escalation weeks → climax + coda [21][22]. Weekly beats used varied media (cutscene, audio, first-person radio, environmental) to prevent format fatigue [22]. Structure per season:

- Weeks 1–3: introduce new NPC faction + mystery
- Weeks 4–8: escalate — character turn, betrayal, tool acquisition
- Weeks 9–12: climactic mission + coda setup for next season
- Inter-season: 1–2 weeks of lore digest and forum discussion

**But** Bungie backed off weekly drip in 2024 because "we wanted to get away from the drip feed" — players wanted to pace themselves [23]. The lesson: **rigid cadence works for communal pacing, hurts solo pacing**. For an authored ongoing game targeting individual play, let the player control the tap but ensure the content *has act structure internally* so each act ends cleanly whether played over one night or one month.

### B8. Yoshida's marathon philosophy [24]
FFXIV's Yoshida plans expansions 2–2.5 years ahead and explicitly frames MMO development as a marathon: "sprinting at top speed the entire time would mean exhausting themselves midway" [24]. He also names the core tension:

> "The same cycle repeat[ing] itself brings about both predictability and boredom... stability creates a sense of security but there is a very fine line between that and boredom." [24]

His team's answer: **stable cadence + deliberately placed surprise beats** ("small but big surprises") embedded in an otherwise predictable patch rhythm [24]. Players learn the rhythm, relax into it, and the surprises hit harder because the baseline is flat.

**Pattern:** for the UOR redesign, commit to a predictable *structural* cadence (weekly frame, monthly arc close, seasonal act break), then hide 1–2 unannounced beats per cycle that break pattern. The predictability earns the surprise.

### B9. Koster on treadmills — the dodge is player-created content [15]
Koster's "game without treadmills" argues that any purely authored progression system eventually exhausts. His preferred fix: **make the player the content generator** (social systems, building, economies, creative expression) so that the authored layer sets the stage and the players fill it indefinitely [15]. This is the Ultima Online / Star Wars Galaxies move.

**Pattern for UOR:** even a single-player authored game can apply this via *character-as-content* — the player's accumulated trait history, relationship log, and decision record become replay material the player re-reads, screenshots, shares. The authored system's job is to *format* the player's history into something narratable.

## Deep Read Notes

### Fåhraeus, GDC 2014 — "Emergent Stories in CK2" [6][7]
Key claims, best reconstructable from secondary coverage since the PDF is image-heavy:
- CK2 designs events as **chained sets with narrative directionality** — each event declares follow-on events it can trigger, often with cooldowns per chain type.
- The game's emergent quality comes less from any single chain and more from **chain collisions** — two unrelated chains accidentally firing on the same character in the same month (the "Satan's daughter" anecdote, where a romance chain collided with a religious-crisis chain) [7].
- Fåhraeus's articulated principle: **emergence = scripted content × simulation collisions × player pattern-finding** [7].
- Future-work item from the talk: **"emergence detection"** — a meta-layer that notices these collisions and amplifies them via music, text variants, and reweighting of subsequent events [6][7]. This is the pattern A4 above.

### Short, "Beyond Branching" [17]
The 2016 piece is still the definitive taxonomy of non-tree narrative structures. The three distinctions matter for UOR:
- QBN: player picks from all eligible — gives feeling of agency but requires careful quality bookkeeping.
- SBN: system picks most relevant — cheaper to author incrementally, because adding content doesn't require re-balancing eligibility.
- Waypoint: engine steers back to authored beats — useful for hybrid "we want drift but also want climaxes to land."

For UOR's "ongoing authored game" target, **hybrid QBN-for-exploration + Waypoint-for-arc-closure** is the idiomatic choice. Most Fallen London–style games use exactly this.

### Short, "Storylets: You Want Them" [18]
Definition: `storylet = content + prerequisites + effects`. Three claims worth stealing:
1. Storylets are "atomic" — authored separately, loaded into a pool, recombined at runtime. This means **a writer can add one storylet at a time without testing the whole graph**. Enormous content-ops advantage for an ongoing game.
2. Storylets compose with game systems (skills, resources, reputations, locations). Any system quality becomes a prerequisite expression.
3. The model scales: "games of any size, meant to last for years, to include DLC." DLC is just new storylets with higher-quality prerequisites.

### Booth, "AI Systems of L4D" [8]
The Director has three explicit states: **Build Up, Peak, Sustain Peak, Peak Fade, Relax** (the paper splits Peak in two). Each has entry/exit conditions based on an aggregate "survivor intensity" score derived from damage taken, ammo burned, movement speed, and proximity to teammates. **Relax is a hard-enforced phase** — specials stop spawning — regardless of how well the players are doing. This is the authored-relief pattern codified as a state machine.

### Cook, "Loops and Arcs" [20]
Core framing: loops = mental model iteration; arcs = one-shot consumption. Sandwich patterns: arc-loop-arc (tutorial → grind → boss). Nested: loop-within-arc (combat rounds in a mission). **Takeaway for ongoing narrative:** the authored story is the arc layer, but it *must* sit atop a loop that doesn't require story to remain engaging, or the game dies when arcs run out.

### RimWorld wiki — Cassandra's curve [27]
- Cassandra: classic three-act pacing with forced recovery between threat events.
- Phoebe Chillax: longer recovery windows, fewer events per cycle.
- Randy Random: abandons dramatic curve; pure noise — included as the "no pacing" baseline.
- All three storytellers read "Storyteller Wealth" (colony net worth converted to raid points). Threat scales with success, not time.
- Storyteller selection is presented to the player at game start as a *pacing preference*, not a difficulty — "how do you want your story told?" is the first UX question. Good UOR idea.

### Fallen London menaces [28]
Four menaces: Nightmares, Suspicion, Wounds, Scandal. Each maxes at 8. Hitting max triggers a forced location change to a penalty zone. Penalty zones have their own storylet pool themed around the menace (asylum for Nightmares, prison for Suspicion). This means **a player who pushes too hard in any direction automatically gets a change-of-scene storylet cluster for free** — pacing, worldbuilding, and mechanics all in one.

### Soap opera craft [25][26]
- Episode-level device: cliffhanger every episode. Revelation, kiss, slap, gunshot, left-unanswered question.
- Arc-level device: interlocking storylines so one resolution triggers another's inciting incident.
- Show-level device: social-issue substitution (Nixon's AIDS, abortion, Vietnam storylines) as renewable dramatic fuel — the show never ages out of topics because the topic pool is the news.
- Head-writer method: story bible first, then monthly breakdowns, then weekly scripts. **Bible + monthly + weekly is the authored analogue of Yoshida's 2.5-year / expansion / patch pyramid.**
