A story is a change

Why “this thing happened” bores a room, and the one moment that fixes it.

Today’s win: you will find the turning moment in a real story, and write the first line of one of your own.

The idea

Most stories told at dinner are really anecdotes: a funny or strange thing happened, the end. The Moth — the stage where thousands of true stories have been told live — draws the line cleanly: an anecdote is a single moment; a story has an arc, with something at stake and someone changed by the end.

NPR Life Kit interview with The Moth’s executive producer: “How to tell a captivating story” (2022).

An anecdote reports what happened.
A story shows who you were before and who you were after.

Matthew Dicks, a many-time Moth StorySLAM winner, names the hinge: the five-second moment — the brief instant when your understanding of something shifts. A story has one. And it can be tiny: in his words, “small is great.”

Matthew Dicks, Storyworthy (2018); his method in his own words: Lenny’s Newsletter interview.

The trick that follows

If the story is the change, the beginning is the opposite of the five-second moment. Dicks builds every story backward: find the moment, then open on the version of you who doesn’t know it yet. Everything between exists to get from one to the other.

Dicks, Storyworthy, “start with the opposite”; summarized in SuperSummary.

Moment: “I realized my father was scared too.”
Opening: “My father wasn’t afraid of anything.”

And without stakes — something that could be won or lost — there is nothing to change. The Moth puts it bluntly: “A story without stakes is an essay.”

Quoted from The Moth’s Storytelling Tips in Nieman Storyboard.

See it work

A short told story. Read it once, then answer question 5.

  1. For ten years I told everyone my brother was the lazy one.
  2. He skipped college, moved back home, and slept until noon.
  3. Last winter Dad had his stroke, and I flew in for two days.
  4. On the second night I found my brother’s notebook on the kitchen table: every pill, every dose, every hour, for three years.
  5. I flew home, and I’ve called him every Sunday since.

Notice line 1. It is the exact opposite of what the narrator learns. That is not luck; it is the trick.

Your drill

Write it, send it to your teacher in chat

  1. Think of one moment from your own life when you understood something differently. Small is fine: a meal, a text, a look.
  2. Write it in one sentence, starting “I realized…”
  3. Write the opening line: the opposite of that realization.

Paste both lines into the chat. You’ll get feedback on whether it’s a real turn and whether the opening sets it up.

Tonight: Homework for Life

Dicks’s daily habit: at the end of each day, ask which moment was most storyworthy and write it down in a sentence or two. Five minutes. It is how you build a stock of five-second moments.

Described in Art of Manliness: “How to tell better stories” and Storyworthy.
