The idea

The Moth gives one hard rule about memorizing: don’t memorize your story, except the first and last lines. Decide your last line before you start telling, so you never wander looking for a way out.

The Moth, How to Tell a Story (2022), per Readingraphics’ summary; “don’t memorize” also in Garden & Gun and NPR Life Kit.

Their advice for that line: land somewhere specific and unique to you, and don’t waste it on a broad concept. A strong ending is rooted in the change you went through.

Three ways to end the same story

Your paneer roll story, three endings. The details are invented; judge the shape.

Ending A

  1. And that’s when I learned that money is meant to be enjoyed.

Ending B

  1. Anyway, then I slept, and next morning I had class, and my roommate was also hungry, so…

Ending C

  1. I still have the spreadsheet. There’s a row for that night: paneer roll, ₹120. In the notes column I wrote one word: worth it.

A is the broad concept the Moth warns about: true of anyone, so it belongs to no one. B wanders past the turn. C shows the after-state with a detail only you have, and it brings the spreadsheet from your first line back, changed. The audience works out the lesson; you never say it.

Check yourself

Your drill

Part 1 · Write the last line

One or two sentences that show who you are after that night. A real detail: what you do now, what you order, what the spreadsheet looks like. Don’t state the lesson.

Part 2 · Say it out loud

  1. Memorize two things only: your first line and your new last line.
  2. Open your phone’s voice recorder. Tell the whole story once, start to finish, without reading. Don’t stop to fix mistakes.
  3. Note the time.

Send in chat: your last line, how long it took, and the one moment where you felt yourself stumble.
